# PART 1: THE ACTUAL PROBLEM WE'RE SOLVING

## What Are "Hard Decisions"?

A Fortune 500 CEO's hardest decisions aren't "should we use blue or green in the logo." They're:

| Decision | Stakes | Current Approach | Time | Cost |
|----------|--------|------------------|------|------|
| "Should we acquire Company X for $2B?" | Career-defining | Bankers + consultants + gut | 6 months | $5M+ |
| "Should we enter the China market?" | $500M+ investment | Internal strategy + BCG | 4 months | $2M |
| "Should we raise prices 15%?" | $200M revenue risk | Survey + analysis | 3 months | $500K |
| "How do we respond to this disruption?" | Existential | War room + intuition | Weeks | Priceless |
| "Should we restructure the sales org?" | 500 jobs | HR analysis + politics | 2 months | Internal |

**The common thread**: High stakes, high uncertainty, insufficient data, political complexity, time pressure.

## Why Current Solutions Fail

### Consultants (McKinsey, BCG, Bain)
- **Good at**: Frameworks, analysis, credibility
- **Bad at**: Speed, specificity, adaptation
- **Output**: "Here are 3 options with pros and cons"
- **Gap**: Still requires executive judgment on WHICH option

### Market Research (Nielsen, Ipsos)
- **Good at**: Representative data, methodology rigor
- **Bad at**: Speed, depth, "why" questions
- **Output**: "67% of respondents said X"
- **Gap**: Doesn't tell you what to DO with that information

### Internal Analysis
- **Good at**: Context, political awareness
- **Bad at**: Objectivity, scope, rigor
- **Output**: Whatever the boss wants to hear
- **Gap**: Confirmation bias, limited perspective

### Gut Instinct
- **Good at**: Speed, conviction
- **Bad at**: Accuracy, defensibility
- **Output**: "I've been in this industry 30 years..."
- **Gap**: Not scalable, not defensible, often wrong

## What Would ACTUALLY Help?

An executive facing a hard decision needs:

1. **Exploration**: "What are ALL the options I haven't considered?"
2. **Simulation**: "What happens if I choose A vs B vs C over the next 12 months?"
3. **Understanding**: "WHY does A lead to outcome X?"
4. **Sensitivity**: "What assumptions matter most?"
5. **Contingency**: "What could change the answer?"
6. **Confidence**: "How sure should I be?"
7. **Action**: "Exactly what do I do, when?"
8. **Defense**: "How do I explain this to my board?"

**No current solution provides ALL of these.**

## Decisions Unfold Over Time

This is critical. Decisions aren't instantaneous. They play out:

```
TIME DYNAMICS OF A PRICE INCREASE DECISION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Day 0:     DECISION - Announce 10% price increase
              │
Day 1-7:   AWARENESS - Customers learn about change
              │  ├─ Email announcement (40% open)
              │  ├─ Account manager calls (high-value)
              │  └─ Word of mouth begins
              │
Day 7-30:  EVALUATION - Customers decide how to respond
              │  ├─ 60% accept without action
              │  ├─ 25% evaluate alternatives
              │  └─ 15% immediately upset
              │
Day 14:    COMPETITOR RESPONSE - Rivals react
              │  ├─ Competitor A: No change
              │  ├─ Competitor B: Launches "Switch & Save"
              │  └─ Competitor C: Matches increase
              │
Day 30-60: CHURN WAVE - Departures happen
              │  ├─ Price-sensitive segment: 15% leave
              │  ├─ Mid-market: 5% leave
              │  └─ Enterprise: 2% leave
              │
Day 45:    SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS - Ripples spread
              │  ├─ Negative reviews appear
              │  ├─ Sales team morale affected
              │  └─ Remaining customers question loyalty
              │
Day 60-90: STABILIZATION - New equilibrium emerges
              │  ├─ Churn slows
              │  ├─ New customer acquisition adjusted
              │  └─ Unit economics recalculated
              │
Day 90+:   OUTCOME - Net result measurable
               ├─ Net revenue change: +X% or -Y%
               ├─ Customer base change: -Z%
               └─ Competitive position: Better/Worse/Same
```

**This temporal unfolding is what simulation captures that surveys cannot.**

## Decisions Have Causal Chains

Every outcome has a traceable cause:

```
CAUSAL CHAIN: Why Did Sarah Churn?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ROOT CAUSE: Price increase announced
    │
    ├─► Sarah sees email (Day 3)
    │       └─► Reaction: "That's a big jump"
    │
    ├─► Sarah checks her usage (Day 4)
    │       └─► Realization: "I only use 30% of features"
    │
    ├─► Sarah Googles alternatives (Day 5)
    │       └─► Discovery: "[Competitor] is $20/mo cheaper"
    │
    ├─► Sarah sees competitor ad (Day 8)
    │       └─► Trigger: "Switch & Save - 3 months free"
    │
    ├─► Sarah asks colleague (Day 10)
    │       └─► Validation: "Yeah, I switched. It's fine."
    │
    ├─► Sarah starts competitor trial (Day 12)
    │       └─► Experience: "This works for my needs"
    │
    └─► Sarah cancels (Day 18)
            └─► Gone: $1,200/year LTV lost

COUNTERFACTUAL: What Would Have Kept Sarah?
    │
    ├─► If price increase was 5% instead of 10%?
    │       └─► Probably stays (below pain threshold)
    │
    ├─► If she was grandfathered for 6 months?
    │       └─► Probably stays (loyalty recognized)
    │
    ├─► If we showed her ROI data?
    │       └─► Maybe stays (value justification)
    │
    └─► If competitor hadn't run promo?
            └─► Maybe stays (no easy alternative)
```

**This is what executives need to see - the CHAIN of causation, not just the outcome.**

# PART 3: WHAT MAKES AGENTS REAL

## The Problem with Fake Personas

Most "synthetic personas" are just:
- Demographics (age, income, location)
- Segment labels (price-sensitive, early adopter)
- Generic preferences

**This produces generic, predictable, useless results.**

Real people are:
- Contradictory (want cheap AND premium)
- Context-dependent (different at work vs home)
- Influenced by relationships (what did my friend buy?)
- Emotional (brands trigger feelings)
- Cognitive misers (use shortcuts, not full analysis)

## The Stanford Architecture (Why It Works)

The Stanford Generative Agents paper (Park et al., 2023) introduced a breakthrough:

### 1. Memory Stream
```
Everything the agent experiences is stored:
    │
    ├─ "Saw ad for Product X on Instagram" (importance: 3)
    ├─ "Friend mentioned switching to Competitor Y" (importance: 7)
    ├─ "Received price increase email" (importance: 8)
    ├─ "Read negative review on Reddit" (importance: 5)
    └─ "Customer service was unhelpful" (importance: 9)

Memories have:
    • Timestamp (when did it happen?)
    • Description (what happened?)
    • Importance (how significant? 1-10)
    • Embedding (semantic representation for retrieval)
```

### 2. Reflection
```
Periodically, agent synthesizes memories into insights:
    │
    Input: Last 50 memories with accumulated importance > 100
    │
    Process: "What are the 3 most salient high-level insights?"
    │
    Output:
    ├─ "I'm increasingly frustrated with the product's value"
    ├─ "My social circle is shifting to alternatives"
    └─ "The company doesn't seem to care about loyal customers"
    │
    These reflections become new memories (recursive!)
```

### 3. Planning
```
Agent generates action plans based on state:
    │
    Given: Goals, constraints, current beliefs
    │
    Output: Hierarchical plan
    │
    Day plan:
    ├─ "Review my subscriptions today"
    ├─ "Research alternatives during lunch"
    └─ "Make decision by end of week"
    │
    Plans adapt when new information arrives
```

**This creates coherent, believable agents that evolve over time.**

## Beyond Stanford: What Else Agents Need

### Belief State (Bayesian)
```
Agent has probabilistic beliefs about the world:
    │
    P(Product X is best value) = 0.7
    P(Competitor Y is reliable) = 0.4
    P(Price will increase again) = 0.6
    │
    When new evidence arrives, beliefs update:
    │
    Evidence: "Friend had bad experience with Competitor Y"
    New: P(Competitor Y is reliable) = 0.25
    │
    This affects decisions probabilistically, not deterministically
```

### Utility Function
```
Agent optimizes for their personal values:
    │
    U(decision) = w1*price + w2*quality + w3*convenience + w4*status
    │
    Different segments have different weights:
    │
    Price-sensitive:  U = 0.6*price + 0.2*quality + 0.15*convenience + 0.05*status
    Premium buyer:    U = 0.1*price + 0.5*quality + 0.2*convenience + 0.2*status
    Convenience-first: U = 0.2*price + 0.2*quality + 0.5*convenience + 0.1*status
```

### Social Network
```
Agents influence each other:
    │
    Sarah ──knows── Mike (colleague, trust: 0.8)
    Sarah ──knows── Lisa (friend, trust: 0.9)
    Sarah ──follows── TechReviewer (influencer, trust: 0.5)
    │
    When Mike switches to competitor:
    ├─ Sarah's P(switching) increases
    ├─ Lisa hears about it through Sarah
    └─ Network effect cascades
```

### Decision Heuristics
```
Real people don't optimize - they satisfice:
    │
    Heuristics:
    ├─ Anchoring: First price seen becomes reference
    ├─ Loss aversion: Losses feel 2x worse than gains
    ├─ Status quo bias: Inertia unless pushed
    ├─ Social proof: "If others are doing it..."
    ├─ Recency: Recent experiences weighted more
    └─ Availability: What comes to mind easily
    │
    These create realistic, imperfect decisions
```
